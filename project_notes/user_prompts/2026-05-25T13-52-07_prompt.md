\begin{abstract}
We present a smart contract architecture for a \textit{Trading and Settlement System} (TSS), deployed on a permissioned blockchain (Hyperledger Besu, IBFT 2.0), designed to operate under the \textit{Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation} (MiCA) and the \textit{Distributed Ledger Technology} (DLT) Pilot Regime. The system comprises a smart contract suite implementing identity verification, asset tokenization, and settlement, featuring a pluggable matching engine interface that supports both a traditional order book and an \textit{Automated Market Maker} (AMM) as interchangeable execution models, with eIDAS-compatible onboarding. This enables fully on-chain trading with atomic \textit{delivery-versus-payment} (DvP) settlement. A proof-of-concept implementation demonstrates the feasibility of the proposed architecture, facilitating multilateral negotiation and settlement while combining regulatory compliance with the transparency benefits of DLT.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Ethereum's programmable smart contracts have enabled a new class of decentralized applications spanning finance, supply chain, and governance.
Decentralized Finance (DeFi)~\cite{Zetzsche2020Decentralized} is the result of applying smart contracts to finance, creating financial services operating on blockchain networks. Typically, it allows users to lend, borrow, trade, manage assets, and access derivatives. However, these systems have limitations. Among them are the lack of regulation, technical vulnerabilities, and lack of trust in platforms. This makes DeFi a niche instead of an established mainstream area of finance.

With DeFi's increase in popularity concerns about investor protection and financial risks prompted European regulators to establish frameworks addressing these challenges~\cite{Werbach2018Trust}. Within the EU, two frameworks have been defined to establish financial regulation for blockchain: MiCA~\cite{EU2023MiCA}, and DLT Pilot Regime~\cite{EU2022DLTPilot}. The goal of both sets of regulations is to allow trustworthy and compliant blockchain-based financial services.

Consequently, there is a growing need for regulation-compliant innovation within DeFi, that rivals the stability and established trust of Traditional Finance (TradFi). This paper details the design and implementation of such a system. The proposed TSS distinguishes itself from existing solutions by combining regulatory compliance through eIDAS-compatible Know Your Customer (KYC) and permissioned access with the transparency and front-running resistance of decentralized execution, addressing the trust and regulatory gaps that limit current DeFi adoption by established institutions. A key architectural contribution is a pluggable matching engine interface that supports interchangeable execution models, namely a traditional order book and an AMM, enabling comparison of different trading mechanisms under the same compliance infrastructure. The system is implemented as a proof-of-concept prototype to demonstrate the feasibility of this approach.

The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Design and implement a permissioned blockchain-based TSS that enforces regulatory compliance through smart contracts and identity verification
    \item Develop a fully on-chain trading and settlement mechanism ensuring atomicity, transparency, and auditability
    \item Design a pluggable matching engine interface supporting interchangeable execution models, namely a traditional order book and an AMM, enabling comparison of different trading mechanisms under the same compliance infrastructure
    \item Integrate eIDAS-compatible identity verification and deploy a proof-of-concept prototype on Hyperledger Besu with IBFT 2.0 consensus, demonstrating feasibility and regulatory suitability
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:implementation} describes the implementation. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} evaluates the system. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background \& Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
\subsection{DeFi Trading Mechanisms}
\label{subsec:defi_trading}
Automated Market Makers (AMMs) replace traditional order books with liquidity pools, enabling trades to execute against pooled reserves rather than requiring a counterparty. Uniswap's constant-product invariant ($x \cdot y = k$) determines prices algorithmically from reserve ratios~\cite{Adams2020UniswapV2}. However, large trades cause significant slippage as they shift the reserve ratio along the pricing curve. AMMs perform no internal price discovery, relying on external arbitrageurs. Their public mempool visibility exposes traders to Maximal Extractable Value (MEV) extraction and sandwich attacks~\cite{Daian2020Flash}. Critically, AMMs operate permissionlessly with no identity verification or regulatory controls.

On-chain order-book decentralized exchanges (DEXs) replicate traditional bid/ask matching with price-time priority directly on the blockchain. However, each order placement and cancellation requires a separate transaction, making gas costs prohibitive under high market activity, while block confirmation latency prevents real-time market making~\cite{Gervais2016Security}. Furthermore, mempool transparency enables validators to reorder transactions for profit, undermining fair price formation~\cite{Daian2020Flash}. Hybrid models address these limitations by performing order matching off-chain and subsequently settling trades on-chain. The 0x protocol broadcasts signed orders via off-chain relayers, submitting only matched trades for on-chain settlement~\cite{Warren2017ZeroX}. Correspondingly, dYdX employs a centralized off-chain matching engine, achieving high throughput but introducing a single point of failure~\cite{Juliano2017dYdX}. Moreover, although these designs approach centralized-exchange performance, their off-chain components introduce trust assumptions, and neither implements identity verification or regulatory compliance. In summary, existing DeFi trading mechanisms either sacrifice performance for decentralization or sacrifice transparency for throughput, and none integrate regulatory compliance.

\subsection{Regulatory Frameworks}
\label{subsec:regulatory_frameworks}
The Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA)~\cite{EU2023MiCA} establishes a comprehensive regulatory framework for crypto-asset service providers across the EU, defining authorization requirements and consumer protection measures. The DLT Pilot Regime~\cite{EU2022DLTPilot} creates a regulatory sandbox for DLT-based multilateral trading facilities (MTFs) and trading and settlement systems (TSSs), specifically targeting tokenized securities. The eIDAS regulation~\cite{EU2014eIDAS} provides a framework for electronic identification and trust services, enabling cross-border recognition of digital identities through qualified electronic signatures and trust service providers. Finally, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)~\cite{EU2016GDPR} constrains blockchain system design, as data immutability conflicts with the right to erasure, thus requiring that only attestation hashes, rather than personally identifiable information, be stored on-chain. 

\subsection{Related Work \& Positioning}
Vaziry et al.~\cite{Vaziry2026KnowYourContract} propose an architecture that cryptographically binds smart contracts to qualified electronic seals issued under eIDAS, establishing a verifiable chain of trust for on-chain identities. However, their scope is limited to the identity and trust layer, addressing who is interacting on-chain but not how trading, matching, or settlement should be implemented. dYdX v4 migrated to a sovereign Cosmos-based blockchain where validators participate in both consensus and order matching, achieving decentralized high-performance trading~\cite{Juliano2017dYdX}. Crucially, it operates outside EU jurisdiction, implements no compliance layer, and targets permissionless derivatives as opposed to regulated securities. No existing system combines a pluggable matching engine interface, demonstrated here with order-book and AMM implementations but extensible to additional strategies, with fully on-chain execution within a permissioned setting, integrated KYC/eIDAS-compatible identity verification, and atomic delivery-versus-payment settlement. This paper addresses that gap.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:system_architecture}
\subsection{Design Principles \& Overview}
The architecture is governed by four design principles derived from the regulatory requirements of the DLT Pilot Regime and the limitations of existing platforms identified in Section \ref{sec:background}. \textbf{Fully on-chain execution.} All trading and settlement logic executes deterministically on the blockchain. This eliminates information asymmetry between the operator and participants, prevents operator front-running, and guarantees that regulators can verify the exact matching logic by reading contract code. Unlike hybrid models such as 0x~\cite{Warren2017ZeroX} and dYdX~\cite{Juliano2017dYdX}, which rely on off-chain matching engines that introduce trust assumptions and single points of failure, the proposed system requires no trusted off-chain component for trade execution. \textbf{Permissioned infrastructure.} The system deploys on Hyperledger Besu~\cite{HyperledgerBesu} with IBFT 2.0 consensus~\cite{IBFT2}, providing instant finality, operator-controlled gas costs, and the ability to enforce KYC at the network level. Permissionless blockchains cannot enforce identity verification, provide emergency intervention capabilities, or guarantee predictable performance, which are all requirements under the DLT Pilot Regime~\cite{EU2022DLTPilot}. \textbf{Separation of concerns.} The architecture strictly decouples matching from settlement, compliance, and token logic through Solidity interfaces. The settlement contract interacts with any matching engine exclusively through the IDvPTrigger interface and is agnostic to the underlying execution model. This pluggable design is the system's core architectural contribution, enabling different trading mechanisms to be deployed, compared, or replaced without modifying the settlement, identity, or token contracts. \textbf{Compliance by construction.} Whitelisting is verified at three stages: when a trader submits an order, when orders are matched, and when the settlement contract executes the atomic transfer. Attestations carry expiry timestamps, forcing periodic re-verification. Only cryptographic attestation hashes are stored on-chain, maintaining GDPR~\cite{EU2016GDPR} compliance while bridging eIDAS-verified identities~\cite{EU2014eIDAS} to blockchain addresses. 

The system comprises four layers. Layer 1: a permissioned Hyperledger Besu network with four IBFT 2.0 validators providing Byzantine fault tolerance. Layer 2: a smart contract suite including identity and whitelisting, an asset registry, ERC-20 token contracts (securities and cash), a settlement contract, and pluggable matching engines (order book and AMM). Layer 3: an off-chain KYC middleware that verifies eIDAS digital signatures and writes attestation hashes on-chain. Layer 4: user and regulator interfaces, including a web-based trading application with MetaMask~\cite{MetaMask} integration and a read-only observer node for regulatory oversight. Five actor roles interact with the system: Trader, Issuer, Operator, Regulator, and KYC Provider.

\subsection{Pluggable Matching Engine Architecture}
\label{subsec:pluggable_matching}

The system's core architectural contribution is a generic interface abstraction that decouples the trading mechanism from the settlement and compliance infrastructure. Any matching engine, whether an order book, an AMM, an auction, or a request-for-quote system, can be deployed behind the same interface without requiring modifications to the settlement, identity, or token contracts. This design follows the strategy pattern: the settlement contract delegates matching to a family of interchangeable algorithms unified by a common interface.
\subsubsection{Interface Abstraction}
The \texttt{IMatchingEngine} interface defines the contract that every trading mechanism must satisfy. It specifies an \texttt{Order} struct containing the trader address, input/output token addresses, amounts, timestamp, and a status enum (\texttt{OPEN}, \texttt{MATCHED}, \texttt{CANCELLED}, \texttt{EXPIRED}); a \texttt{Match} struct recording the matched buyer, seller, tokens, amounts, and timestamp; and four functions: \texttt{submitOrder()}, \texttt{cancelOrder()}, \texttt{getOrder()}, and \texttt{getPendingMatches()}. It is important to note that the interface imposes no assumptions about how or when matching occurs internally. An order book may hold orders indefinitely until a counterparty arrives, while an AMM matches immediately against pooled liquidity. Both satisfy the same interface.
The settlement layer interacts with matching engines exclusively through the \texttt{IDvPTrigger} interface, which exposes a single \texttt{settle()} function accepting buyer, seller, token addresses, and amounts. This separation means the settlement contract contains zero knowledge of order books, pricing curves, or liquidity pools. The \texttt{Settlement} contract maintains an \texttt{\_authorizedEngines} mapping that controls which deployed contracts may invoke settlement. An operator can register or deregister engines at runtime via \texttt{authorizeEngine()} and \texttt{revokeEngine()}, protected by the \texttt{onlyOperator} modifier. This runtime registration model enables hot-swapping of trading mechanisms without contract redeployment: a market operator can revoke an order book, deploy an upgraded version, and authorize it, all while the settlement, identity, and token contracts remain untouched. Regulators can mandate different matching mechanisms for different asset classes by authorizing multiple engines simultaneously, each handling a specific market segment.
\subsubsection{Order Book Implementation}
The \texttt{OrderBook} contract implements \texttt{IMatchingEngine} with price-time priority matching. The order lifecycle proceeds as follows: a whitelisted trader calls \texttt{submitOrder()} specifying input token, output token, and amounts. The contract validates that both tokens are registered and active in the registry, creates the order with status \texttt{OPEN}, appends its identifier to the \texttt{\_openOrderIds} array, and immediately attempts matching via the internal \texttt{\_tryMatch()} function.
The matching loop iterates over all open orders seeking a counterparty whose token pair is the inverse of the new order and whose price crosses. Price crossing is determined via cross-multiplication ($\mathit{amountIn}_{\mathit{buy}} \times \mathit{amountIn}_{\mathit{sell}} \geq \mathit{amountOut}_{\mathit{buy}} \times \mathit{amountOut}_{\mathit{sell}}$), avoiding fixed-point division and the associated rounding errors. Matching complexity is $O(n)$ where $n$ is the number of open orders. The \texttt{\_openOrderIds} array uses a swap-and-pop removal pattern with an auxiliary index mapping (\texttt{\_openOrderIndex}), enabling $O(1)$ removal upon match or cancellation without leaving gaps in the array.
Compliance is enforced at two points: the \texttt{onlyWhitelisted} modifier rejects submissions from non-whitelisted addresses, and the matching loop re-verifies whitelisting for both parties at match time. This double-check prevents a scenario where a trader's attestation expires between order submission and execution. Self-trading is explicitly prevented by skipping candidates with the same trader address. Upon a successful match, the contract determines buyer and seller roles by consulting the registry's asset type classification, updates both orders to \texttt{MATCHED} status, removes them from the open orders array, records the match, and immediately invokes \texttt{Settlement.settle()}, triggering atomic DvP within the same transaction.
\subsubsection{AMM Implementation}
The \texttt{AMM} contract implements the same \texttt{IMatchingEngine} interface using a constant-product invariant ($x \cdot y = k$) with a configurable fee expressed in basis points. Unlike the order book, the AMM produces no resting orders: submission, matching, and settlement occur atomically within a single transaction. The pool itself acts as the counterparty and must be whitelisted as a participant in the identity contract, ensuring that even algorithmic liquidity provision is subject to compliance verification.
The output amount for a given input is computed by the \texttt{getAmountOut()} function, which applies the fee and solves the constant-product equation. A caller-specified \texttt{amountOutMin} parameter provides slippage protection, reverting the transaction if market conditions shift unfavorably between submission and execution. Liquidity providers deposit paired tokens via \texttt{addLiquidity()} and receive shares proportional to their contribution relative to existing reserves. Withdrawal via \texttt{removeLiquidity()} burns shares and returns the proportional reserve amounts.
After computing the output amount and verifying slippage bounds, the contract updates its internal reserves, creates both the order (immediately set to \texttt{MATCHED}) and the match record, and calls \texttt{Settlement.settle()} with the pool address as buyer or seller depending on trade direction. The settlement contract then executes the atomic token transfers, treating the AMM pool identically to any human counterparty.
\subsubsection{Architectural Implications}
Two fundamentally different execution models, a discrete order book with resting orders and price-time priority, and a continuous AMM with algorithmic pricing and pooled liquidity, share identical settlement and compliance infrastructure without any adapter or translation layer. Both engines call the same \texttt{settle()} function with the same parameters; the settlement contract cannot distinguish which engine originated the trade. This demonstrates that the interface abstraction imposes no constraints on the internal matching logic while guaranteeing that every trade, regardless of mechanism, passes through the same compliance gates and settles atomically.
The design is intentionally open to extension. Additional engines, such as periodic call auctions for illiquid securities, bilateral request-for-quote systems for large blocks, or Dutch auctions for initial token offerings, can be added by implementing \texttt{IMatchingEngine}, deploying the contract, and registering it via \texttt{authorizeEngine()}. No existing contract requires modification or redeployment.

\subsection{Compliance \& Identity Layer}
\label{subsec:compliance_identity}
The \texttt{Identity} contract implements role-based access control with four roles: \texttt{TRADER}, \texttt{ISSUER}, \texttt{OPERATOR}, and \texttt{REGULATOR}. The KYC Provider, while a system actor, operates exclusively off-chain and interacts with the contract through a privileged attestation write function rather than holding an on-chain role. Each whitelisted address is assigned exactly one role and an attestation with an expiry timestamp, forcing periodic re-verification. This ensures that stale identities cannot participate in trading: if a KYC attestation expires, the address is effectively excluded from all system operations until re-verified.
Integration with eIDAS~\cite{EU2014eIDAS} is achieved through an off-chain KYC middleware. The system integrates with an eIDAS Identity Provider that verifies legal identities using national ID cards through digital signatures. The KYC service provider, a licensed entity, performs due diligence to verify the validity of the eIDAS signatures and stores KYC documents in an encrypted database. This middleware is responsible for connecting the off-chain identity to the on-chain address by generating a cryptographic attestation that gets written to the blockchain. Only attestation hashes are stored on-chain; personally identifiable information such as passport scans and physical addresses cannot be stored on an immutable blockchain due to GDPR~\cite{EU2016GDPR} requirements. Instead, only cryptographic attestations proving that KYC was performed are written on-chain, satisfying the right to erasure since the on-chain hash is meaningless without the off-chain mapping.
Compliance is enforced at three independent gates. First, the matching engine's \texttt{onlyWhitelisted} modifier rejects order submissions from non-whitelisted or expired addresses. Second, the matching logic re-checks whitelisting at match time, catching attestations that expired while an order was resting. Third, the \texttt{Settlement} contract verifies both parties' whitelisting status before executing the atomic transfer. This triple-gate design ensures that no trade can complete unless both counterparties hold valid, non-expired attestations at the moment of settlement, regardless of how much time elapsed since order submission.
Administrative control follows a two-step transfer pattern: the current administrator initiates a transfer to a proposed address, and the proposed address must explicitly accept. This prevents accidental or malicious transfer of administrative privileges to an incorrect address.

\subsection{Atomic DvP Settlement}
\label{subsec:dvp_settlement}
The system is designed around delivery-versus-payment (DvP) settlement~\cite{BIS1992DvP}, where securities tokens are exchanged atomically for cash tokens. When compatible orders are matched, the settlement contract is invoked to execute DvP: securities tokens and cash tokens are transferred atomically within a single on-chain transaction, ensuring that either both transfers succeed or both are reverted. This guarantees payment finality and eliminates counterparty risk.
The \texttt{Settlement} contract re-validates compliance at settlement time: both parties must be whitelisted with non-expired attestations, and both tokens must be registered and active in the registry. In the case of any condition failing, the entire transaction reverts, preventing settlement of non-compliant trades even if they passed earlier validation stages. The contract records each settlement with a timestamp and emits a \texttt{SettlementExecuted} event, providing auditability and regulatory visibility. This makes every trade both reproducible and auditable, which in a regulated market is worth the gas costs.
The \texttt{onlyAuthorizedEngine} modifier restricts settlement invocation to registered matching engines, preventing unauthorized contracts or externally owned accounts from triggering token transfers. Combined with the \texttt{onlyOperator}-protected \texttt{authorizeEngine()} function, this creates a two-layer authorization model: the operator controls which engines exist, and the engines control which trades settle.
An emergency pause mechanism allows the operator to halt all settlement activity via a circuit breaker, satisfying the DLT Pilot Regime's~\cite{EU2022DLTPilot} requirement for emergency intervention capabilities. When paused, no new settlements can execute until the operator explicitly resumes operations.
